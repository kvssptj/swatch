# Example run — Model B

- **Date:** 2026-10-03
- **Model:** Model B, fictional example
- **Experiment:** [Write a story from three photos]({{ '/experiments/write-a-story-from-three-photos/' | relative_url }})
- **Experiment version:** 1
- **Tags:** imagination, perception, writing

## Inputs

Both example runs use the same three fictional photos:

1. A red bicycle rests against a closed gas station at dawn.
2. A wet mountain road passes through dark pine trees.
3. An older woman holds a paper map beside a blue station wagon.

## Exact prompt

> Study these three photos. Write a 700-word road-trip story that connects all three scenes. Use visible details from each photo. Do not describe the photos as separate items.

## Raw output

The gas station had been closed long enough for dust to soften the lettering on its windows. June stopped anyway. Dawn had not reached the valley floor, and the fuel gauge in her borrowed station wagon had stayed below empty for the last twelve miles.

A red bicycle waited beside the building. It was clean. The tires were full. No lock held it to the pipe.

June walked around the station and called out. No one answered. She found no fuel, but a narrow road continued behind the building. A small sign showed a tent, a lake, and an arrow. The road was too narrow for the wagon. She left a note under the windshield wiper, took the bicycle, and started to climb.

The first mile was easy. Then the sky closed. Rain struck the road and turned the dust to dark paste. Pine trees pressed close on both sides. June rode until the slope forced her to walk. She had come to the mountains to find a campsite from one of her father's old photographs. The station attendant in the last town had said the lake was somewhere beyond the pass. He had also said the weather would stay clear.

At a bend, June heard a car horn. A blue station wagon stood across the road with its rear wheels in a shallow ditch. An older woman was placing stones behind one tire. A large paper map lay open across the roof and collected rain.

“You chose a poor day for a bicycle,” the woman said.

“You chose a poor place to park.”

The woman smiled and introduced herself as Ruth. Together they moved stones and broken branches under the wheels. June pushed while Ruth used the accelerator. The wagon climbed back onto the road with a spray of mud.

Inside the car, Ruth spread the wet map between them. June showed her the photograph. Ruth turned it once, then pointed to a small mark beside the lake symbol.

“That shelter burned down fifteen years ago,” she said. “But the view is still there.”

The bicycle went into the rear of the wagon. They drove higher as the rain became quieter. Ruth knew the road, but she still checked the map at every fork. June began to understand that the checking was not uncertainty. It was a habit of respect for places that could change.

Near the pass, the clouds opened. Far below them, the closed gas station was a pale square beside the main road. The borrowed wagon still waited there. From this distance, the red bicycle could have been a dropped match.

They reached the lake in the afternoon. The shelter was gone. The mountain behind it was unchanged.

June held up the old photograph. Ruth stood beside her and studied both versions of the view. Neither woman said that they matched. They did not. The trees were taller, the shore was narrower, and two strangers had arrived by a route that the photograph could not show.

## Observations

- The story uses the road and map as continuing parts of the journey.
- The bicycle is visible but less important after the midpoint.
- The model adds unsupported names, locations, and history.
- The ending keeps some uncertainty and avoids a full explanation.
